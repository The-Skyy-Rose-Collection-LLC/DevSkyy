# Fidelity Hardening for the SkyyRose Fashion-Film Pipeline

Research date: 2026-07-20. Scope: the exact pipeline in production — stills via Higgsfield **gpt_image_2** (1k/low), motion via Higgsfield **seedance_2_0** (ByteDance Seedance 2.0, fast mode, 480p, 4s, 9:16, `start_image` conditioning). Goal: prevent the four observed failures — text/lettering degradation, garment design drift, character identity drift, outfit "bleed" — with techniques that are concrete, sourced, and wireable into the existing pipeline without changing its architecture.

Two params tables below (`seedance_2_0`, `gpt_image_2`) were pulled live from the project's own Higgsfield MCP server (`models_explore action:get`, free call) on 2026-07-20 — these are primary-source ground truth for what the API actually accepts, not secondary documentation, and several findings below depend on them directly.

## 1. Text preservation in image-to-video

**The core problem is architectural, not a prompting bug.** Image-to-video diffusion models (Seedance's family included) don't hold the first-frame conditioning at full strength throughout generation — they deliberately *degrade* (blur) the conditioning signal at high noise levels so the model has room to synthesize motion, then relax that degradation as denoising completes. The CVPR 2026 "Adaptive Low-Pass Guidance" work makes this trade-off explicit: "injecting the clean conditioning latent unchanged at all timesteps can make the model over-rely on the first frame as a near-copy target, improving appearance preservation but weakening motion synthesis" — so at high noise levels the conditioning is "maximally blurred," and only at low noise levels does "the first-frame appearance recover, restoring precise identity and texture control near the end of denoising" [20]. Fine, small-scale detail (a few characters of print on a jersey) lives exactly in the frequency range that gets blurred away; large-scale detail (a big stylized script logo covering a third of the garment) survives because it's still recognizable even after low-pass filtering. This is the direct mechanistic explanation for "BLACK IS BEAUTIFUL → BLACKY" vs. "Love Hurts" surviving — it is not model-specific, it's how first-frame-conditioned video diffusion works in general.

**Resolution helps, but doesn't solve it.** ByteDance's Seedance 2.0 is available in `fast` mode (480p/720p only) and `std` mode (480p/720p/1080p/4k) — confirmed directly from the live Higgsfield MCP schema for `seedance_2_0` [live schema, 2026-07-20]. Going from 480p to 720p or 1080p increases the pixel budget for a given text region and will generally improve legibility, but resolution alone doesn't fix the conditioning-degradation mechanism above — it raises the ceiling, it doesn't remove the floor. General video-generation commentary corroborates that text rendering "remains a significant limitation... particularly for small fonts and extended text content" across resolution tiers [see general survey in search: "AI video generation garbled text small font diffusion model resolution"]. There is no authoritative Seedance-specific benchmark quantifying "resolution X guarantees text legible" — treat any resolution-based fix as probabilistic risk reduction, not a guarantee, and pair it with the QC gate in §4.

**Non-generative alternatives (the actually reliable fix):**
- **Compositing the real logo back over the moving garment** — motion-track the garment surface (After Effects/Mocha Pro planar tracking, e.g. Mocha's PowerMesh for "organic or warped surfaces like skin or fabric") and paste the real, pixel-perfect logo/text asset onto the tracked surface in post [community guidance on tracking graphics onto garments: "blend modes can simulate the graphic distorting on folds in the fabric," "adding a graphic is much easier than replacing one because you don't have to generate a clean surface for the new graphic"]. This is strictly the highest-fidelity option because it never asks the generative model to render the text at all.
- **Keeping the camera off the text** — frame shots so small print isn't the hero of a close-up; reserve close-ups for large, stylized elements that survive generation (confirmed by the Love Hurts example). General AI-ad best-practice guidance converges on the same rule: "fine details on packaging — text, logos, small design elements — sometimes get distorted or hallucinated. The practical workaround is to use AI video primarily for atmosphere, motion, and lifestyle framing, rather than close-up detail shots where accuracy matters most."
- **Video inpainting / masked regeneration to protect a fixed region** — diffusion-based video inpainting techniques exist that "preserve normal regions" while only synthesizing a masked area, using spatially variant noise schedules so "only masked regions are synthesized while known regions are preserved" [arxiv 2405.00251 / general video-inpainting literature]. This is the technical basis for a hypothetical "animate everything except the logo region" approach — not directly exposed as a Seedance parameter today, but the closest analogous *self-hosted* technique if a text region needs to stay 100% static while the rest of the garment moves. Given no evidence this is exposed via the Higgsfield API, treat it as a longer-horizon option (custom pipeline), not a near-term lever.
- **Minimal re-rendering of the branded region** — a corollary of the inpainting point: the less the diffusion process needs to hallucinate over the branded area (small motion, no fabric fold crossing the logo, stable camera), the better the odds of survival, because it stays closer to the un-blurred first-frame condition.

**OpenAI gpt_image_2 stills are comparatively strong on text** — this matters because the *starting* still is the highest-fidelity representation of the text the pipeline ever has. Higgsfield's own GPT Image 2 page claims "over 95% text accuracy, including Chinese, Japanese, and Korean characters on curved surfaces, at small sizes, inside dense layouts" [11], and OpenAI's own gpt-image-1.5 cookbook guide recommends spelling out "tricky words (brand names, uncommon spellings)... letter-by-letter to improve character accuracy" and wrapping literal text in quotes/ALL CAPS with explicit typography constraints [5]. **Practical implication for this pipeline**: any text-legibility loss you're seeing is being introduced at the *video* stage, not inherited from the still — so QC and hardening effort should concentrate on the image-to-video hop, and the still itself is a reliable ground-truth to composite/verify against.

## 2. Garment / product fidelity in image-to-video

**Seedance 2.0 has no explicit conditioning-strength parameter and no `negative_prompt` field.** This was verified directly against the live Higgsfield MCP schema, not inferred: `seedance_2_0`'s exposed parameters are `duration`, `resolution`, `mode`, `bitrate_mode`, `genre`, `generate_audio` — none of them control how strongly the `start_image` anchors the output, and there is no `negative_prompt` field at all [live schema, 2026-07-20]. This directly contradicts an assumption the pipeline might otherwise carry over from image-diffusion tooling (where negative prompts are a first-class param). The only levers available are: (a) which reference images/roles are supplied (`start_image`, `end_image`, `image_references`, `video_references`), and (b) the natural-language prompt itself. General community guidance on ByteDance's Seedance products confirms this pattern — negative constraints are written as explicit sentences *inside* the main prompt (e.g. the documented workaround for unwanted subtitles: "closing prompts with explicit constraint sentences like 'Keep it subtitle-free'" rather than a separate negative-prompt field).

**Prompt phrasing that forbids redesign** — converging guidance across OpenAI's own docs and third-party fashion-prompting guides gives a consistent pattern:
- State the preserve-list explicitly and specifically: "preserve the exact product dimensions, proportions, surface materials, label placement, printed text, logo design, and color accuracy" — generic guidance for product accuracy in generative prompts.
- Fal.ai's GPT Image 2 guide (image side, but the discipline transfers) frames this as "separate change from preserve": use "change only X" and "keep everything else the same," and *repeat* the preserve list on every iteration/regeneration because drift compounds across turns [10].
- For product video specifically: "the product should not change shape, label, logo, packaging, material, color, or proportions" and geometry-specific negative-style phrasing works even without a dedicated field — "no geometry distortion, edges remain straight, surface reflections stay stable, shadows maintain consistency, no phantom duplicate objects" (written as prose constraints, since Seedance has no structured negative-prompt slot).

**Reference-image best practice for Seedance specifically** — the model accepts up to 9 reference inputs and a structured `@`-tag reference system in Higgsfield's own tooling: `@character` for face/identity, `@style` for lighting/color/mood, `@motion` for camera behavior from an existing clip, `@audio` for voice/music sync [2]. For garment fidelity, the actionable pattern from Higgsfield's own Elements documentation is to build a dedicated, named product Element from the *real* photo and reference it by name in every generation rather than re-describing the garment in prose each time — this is explicitly framed as the fix for "cross-generation drift," where "a character or product that recurs in every scene must come from ONE locked reference, or the model reinvents it slightly each time" [4]. When an Element should define *appearance only* (not environment), Higgsfield's documented pattern is to say so explicitly in the prompt — e.g. their own worked example: "Appearance, scale-texture and color reference only; ignore the photo's background and lighting, do not use it for the environment" [4]. This is directly applicable to garment Elements: it stops the model pulling in the reference photo's studio background/lighting/pose as unwanted signal.

**A relevant primary-research finding on product fidelity** (Google DeepMind, non-Seedance but architecturally close — diffusion-based product recontextualization): existing personalization methods (DreamBooth-style, InstructPix2Pix-style) applied naively to arbitrary product photos "struggle with intricate details, consistent appearance, and product background disentanglement, especially when dealing with complex textures, reflections, and occlusions," and this gets worse "scaling to a wide variety of products" [6]. Their fix path is a purpose-built data-augmentation and disentanglement pipeline rather than prompting alone — the practical takeaway for this pipeline (which can't retrain Seedance) is that prompting + reference discipline is a *mitigation*, not a guarantee, at the level of texture/reflection/occlusion detail; the QC gate in §4 exists precisely to catch what prompting alone won't.

## 3. Identity preservation across frames (the mascot)

**Three-layer consistency stack, not one mechanism.** Higgsfield's own production guidance (compiled from official blog posts) explicitly recommends stacking three distinct mechanisms because they operate at different scopes [4]:

| Layer | Scope | Mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| `@` references / Elements | Within one generation, reusable via named Elements across a project | Model reads uploaded reference images at generation time |
| Soul ID | Across every generation on the platform, all models | A trained identity layer — internalizes facial structure once from 20+ photos, holds across pose/lighting/style [3] |
| Reference sheets + visual anchors | Prompt-level insurance | Detailed geometric descriptors (3–5 visual anchors per prompt) so text and image agree |

Higgsfield frames the trade-off among the three tiers plainly: "Re-prompting... is fast to start, but the face drifts because the model re-interprets the description every generation. A reference image per generation... [gives] better anchoring, but still drifts across many separate generations. A self-trained LoRA [gives] a reusable identity, but it needs a dataset and technical setup. Soul ID sits between them: more control than a prompt, less overhead than a LoRA" [3]. For a recurring character like the SkyyRose mascot that appears across many separate generations (not just multiple frames of one clip), **Soul ID or an equivalent trained-identity approach is the correct tier** — single-reference-per-generation is documented to drift across *separate* generations even though it holds reasonably well *within* one clip.

**Face-only crops to avoid outfit bleed.** The specific mechanism behind "a reference image's own outfit leaking into the output" has an academic name and root cause: training/conditioning pipelines that don't cleanly separate identity from other visual attributes in the reference image can leak "target information to the conditioning encoder, leading to shortcut learning and spatial information leakage" [19]. The fix path in that research is architectural (train on a *different* reference image of the same identity than the target, "empowers tasks such as composing faces and clothes to change outfits" [19]) — not directly actionable against a closed API, but it validates the practical workaround Higgsfield's own guidance already recommends: **build separate, named Elements per physical/wardrobe state** rather than reusing one photo for both identity and outfit. Their own worked example is instructive — for an athlete going from dry to sweat-soaked, they used *two separate Elements* (`@s_hero` dry, `@s_hero_wet` damp) rather than asking the model to transform one reference, specifically because "baking the physical state into a locked reference prevents face drift during dry-to-wet transitions" [4]. The same logic applies directly to the mascot: if a shot needs the mascot in Outfit B, use a locked `@mascot_outfitB` Element rather than the canonical `@mascot` reference plus a text instruction to change clothes — asking the model to *change* an attribute in one shot invites exactly the leak/bleed failure mode; *swapping to a pre-built reference* for that state does not.

**Cross-frame identity metric, if you want a numeric signal (see also §4):** the research community's standard for measuring face identity stability *within* a generated video is FaceSim-Arc — ArcFace embeddings computed per frame, compared to both the reference image and to each other, using cosine similarity in ArcFace's embedding space; lower cosine distance between adjacent frames indicates better identity stability [18]. A practical implementation detail from adjacent video-identity work: face tracking with InsightFace can maintain a bounding box and "re-inject identity per keyframe approximately every 8–12 frames" for video-to-video pipelines [18] — useful context if a future iteration of the mascot pipeline moves toward multi-shot continuity rather than independent single-clip generations.

## 4. Automated QC — concrete tools and how to wire them

The goal here is to replace "a human eyeballs the output" with a scriptable gate that runs on every generated clip before it reaches a human, catching exactly the four failure classes named in the task.

### 4a. OCR-based text-mangling detector

**Tools:** PaddleOCR or EasyOCR over Tesseract for this use case. PaddleOCR "provides state-of-the-art accuracy for small, pixelated text" and "delivers high accuracy even on low-quality or cluttered images," while EasyOCR is "significantly more robust than Tesseract for 'in-the-wild' text and handles noise and blur much better"; Tesseract by contrast "produces garbled output on anything slightly degraded" and "performs best when document quality is good" — which is precisely the opposite of a video-diffusion output. GPU is recommended for EasyOCR.

**Pipeline:**
1. Extract frames with ffmpeg at a fixed sample rate (e.g. 2–4 fps is enough for a 4s clip) — a documented, standard pattern for this exact task ("video can be segmented into constituent frames using FFmpeg... to transform videos into uniform frame sequences for OCR processing"). ffmpeg also ships a native `ocr` video filter if you want to avoid a separate frame-dump step, though a Python OCR library gives more control over thresholds.
2. Crop/mask to the known garment-text region per shot (from the shot plan/dossier — you already know where the logo is on the source still).
3. Run PaddleOCR/EasyOCR on each cropped frame; collect the recognized string per frame.
4. Compare each recognized string to the **expected string** (e.g. "BLACK IS BEAUTIFUL") using **normalized Levenshtein distance** — this exact approach (edit-distance comparison of OCR'd overlay text against a reference) is documented in the video-text-extraction literature for exactly this kind of QC task [15].
5. Threshold: below a similarity cutoff (e.g. <80% normalized similarity, tune empirically against known-good/known-bad clips) → auto-flag for human review or auto-reject before it reaches a human at all.

**Sizing the gate correctly — the legibility floor.** OCR engines themselves need a minimum character height to work reliably: general guidance across OCR vendor documentation converges on **~25–30 pixels of character height** for Latin-script text to OCR reliably (Cognex machine-vision guidance: "human readable text should be at least 30 pixels tall and 10 pixels wide for OCR"; a separate OCR-vendor guideline: "individual character height... in the range of 25 to 30 pixels" for Western scripts). This is directly useful two ways: (a) it explains *why* "BLACK IS BEAUTIFUL" at 480p likely failed — do the arithmetic on the actual on-screen character height for that shot's framing, and if it's under ~25px, the failure is close to inevitable regardless of prompt tuning; (b) it gives you a **pre-generation gate**, not just a post-generation one — measure expected character height from the shot's planned framing before paying for the Seedance call, and reject/reframe shots that won't clear the floor (this is action #2 in the executive summary).

### 4b. Garment-similarity detector (CLIP / DINO embeddings)

**Tools:** CLIP (OpenAI) as a baseline, fine-tuned CLIP or DINO/DINOv2 for higher precision. Documented performance gap is large and directly relevant: base CLIP "matched correct SKUs just 41% of the time" on a retail benchmark, while a **fine-tuned** CLIP model reached "92.44% top-1 and 99.3% top-3 accuracy" on a 10M+ product dataset. A research note on product-fidelity evaluation specifically names the metric pairing to use: "**CLIP-I and DINO metrics** are used to compare generated images against reference images to evaluate product fidelity, with **SegCLIP-I** providing more localized cosine similarity comparisons" [6] — SegCLIP-I in particular (segment-then-embed) is the right granularity for "does the garment region match," not the whole frame.

**Pipeline:**
1. From the real product photo (your ground truth), compute a CLIP/DINO embedding of the garment region (crop or segment it out first — background-inclusive embeddings dilute the signal).
2. Sample frames from the generated video (e.g. every 0.5s), crop/segment the same garment region (a lightweight detector or even a fixed crop if the shot is stable), embed each.
3. Cosine similarity between the reference embedding and each frame embedding. A sustained drop below a tuned threshold flags **design drift** (invented/altered garment) — this is the automatable version of full-res-QC for garment fidelity, run before a human opens the PNG.
4. If build budget allows, fine-tuning CLIP on your own catalog images (as the Width.ai case study did, going from 41% to 92%+ top-1) is the single highest-leverage investment if garment-similarity false-positive/negative rates from base CLIP prove too noisy in practice.

### 4c. Face-identity detector (ArcFace / InsightFace / DeepFace)

**Tools:** `serengil/deepface` (Python, MIT-licensed, actively maintained) wraps face detection + alignment + embedding + verification in one call, defaulting to VGG-Face + cosine similarity but swappable to ArcFace and other distance metrics. Academic practice for *video* identity consistency specifically uses ArcFace embeddings (not just any face model) computed per-frame, compared "to the reference image and... across frames using the ArcFace embedding space" — named FaceSim-Arc in the literature [18].

**Pipeline:**
1. Compute a reference ArcFace embedding from the mascot's canonical reference image (per project memory: `assets/branding/mascot/skyy-canonical-reference.jpeg` — never a generated render, per the existing `bug-268` rule).
2. Sample frames from the output clip, run face detection + ArcFace embedding per frame (`DeepFace.verify()` with `model_name="ArcFace"`, `distance_metric="cosine"`).
3. Two checks: (a) reference-to-frame similarity (is this still recognizably the mascot?), (b) frame-to-frame similarity (is identity stable across the clip, or does it drift mid-shot?). Both are cheap — one forward pass per sampled frame.
4. Threshold both; flag on either failing.

### 4d. Wiring it together

All three checks (OCR/text, CLIP-garment, ArcFace-identity) are independent, cheap (CPU-feasible for OCR/CLIP, GPU-preferred but not required for ArcFace at low frame-sample-rates), and can run as a single post-generation script: `ffmpeg -> frame samples -> {ocr_check, clip_check, face_check} -> pass/flag/reject`. None of this requires paid API calls — it's the automatable pre-filter that should run on every Seedance output *before* a human opens the full-res PNG for the founder-mandated visual QC pass, catching the mechanical failure classes (mangled text, drifted garment, drifted face) so human attention concentrates on subjective/aesthetic judgment instead of pixel-counting.

## 5. Higgsfield / Seedance-specific notes (verified against live API, not inferred)

Pulled directly from `mcp__higgsfield__models_explore(action:"get", model_id:"seedance_2_0")` and `(model_id:"gpt_image_2")` on 2026-07-20 — this is the ground truth for what the pipeline's actual API surface supports, superseding any general web guidance where they conflict:

**`seedance_2_0` (ByteDance, via Higgsfield):**
- Parameters: `duration` (4–15s, default 5), `resolution` (480p/720p/1080p/4k, default 720p), `mode` (`std` = higher quality, supports all four resolutions; `fast` = cheaper/faster, **480p/720p only**), `bitrate_mode` (standard/high), `genre` (cinematic hint), `generate_audio` (bool).
- Media roles: `start_image`, `end_image`, `image_references`, `video_references`, `audio_references`.
- **No `negative_prompt` parameter. No explicit reference/conditioning-strength parameter.** Both of these have to be handled through prompt phrasing and choice of reference images — confirmed directly, not assumed (see §2 for why this matters).
- Current pipeline config (fast/480p/4s) is the *cheapest and most text-lossy* corner of this parameter space. `std` mode unlocks 720p/1080p/4k at presumably higher cost — get a `get_cost:true` preflight (free) before committing to a resolution bump, per this project's standing paid-call gate; do not assume the verified fast-tier costs (480p/4s=4cr, fast/720p=6cr per project memory) carry over to `std` mode.

**`gpt_image_2` (OpenAI, via Higgsfield):**
- Parameters: `resolution` (1k/2k/4k, default 1k), `quality` (low/medium/high, default low).
- Media role: `image` only (no start/end/reference-image role split like Seedance).
- Tags include `text-rendering` and `typography` explicitly — consistent with Higgsfield's own marketing claim of "over 95% text accuracy... at small sizes, inside dense layouts" [11] and with the current pipeline's use of 1k/low for stills, which is the cheapest tier; if a still's source text is marginal even before video, bumping `quality` (not just `resolution`) may be the more relevant lever, since `quality` is described as controlling rendering care/detail rather than just pixel count.

**Consistency stack (Elements + Soul ID + reference sheets)** is documented across multiple official Higgsfield blog posts (Soul ID guide, the Elements/consistency guide, the Seedance 2.0 tutorial's `@` reference system) and is consistent across sources — see §3 for the full breakdown. One thing worth flagging: none of the official Higgsfield material found in this research quantifies text-in-video legibility by resolution tier, or the strength of `start_image` conditioning, in numeric terms — where this report gives numeric thresholds (e.g. the ~25–30px OCR legibility floor), they come from general OCR/machine-vision literature, not a Seedance-specific benchmark. Treat those numbers as a planning heuristic, not a guarantee, and let the automated QC gate in §4 be the actual arbiter for any real shot.
